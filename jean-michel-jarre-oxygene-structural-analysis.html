Timestamp-Based Structural Analysis

Oxygène

Artist: Jean-Michel Jarre
Released: December 1976
Total Length: 39:39
Tracks: 6
Producer: Jean-Michel Jarre

Overview

Recorded in Jarre's Paris apartment kitchen on Rue de la Trémoille — no studio time, no recording budget, no label commission. Six continuous parts that function as a single atmospheric statement about air, breath, and the texture of space. Key equipment: ARP 2600, EMS Synthi AKS, EMS VCS 3, Farfisa Professional Organ, Eminent 310 Unique (the atmospheric string sound), Korg Mini-Pops 7 drum machine, RMI Harmonic Synthesizer, Mellotron, and Michel Geiss's custom Rhytmi-computer. Produced and mixed with Jean-Pierre Janiaud.

Released December 1976 on Disques Motors / Polydor (France); initially 50,000 copies given free to hi-fi shops as a demonstration record. Grew to 15–18 million copies worldwide. Oxygène Part IV became the UK hit single. Won the Grand Prix du Disque. Timestamps approximate to ±5 seconds given the continuous-tape nature of the album's original recording.

02 Oxygène Part II 7:49
Energy: Mid Iconic Melody ARP 2600 Sequence Side One Centrepiece
Album role: The album's defining statement — the most recognised synthesizer melody of the 1970s. The arpeggiated ARP 2600 sequence over the Eminent 310 chord pad. Where Part I prepared, Part II delivers. The cultural centre of gravity for all electronic music in France.
0:00 The melodic sequence enters — the famous Oxygène Part II arpeggiated riff. A looping melodic pattern on the ARP 2600 sequencer, immediately recognisable. This single sequence launched an entire genre of electronic music. 1976 France: no precedent for an album-length purely instrumental synthesizer work selling at this scale.
1:00 Harmonic movement underneath — the Eminent 310 pads shift chord, transforming the sequence's emotional character with each change. The genius of Part II: the same sequence sounds completely different as the harmonic foundation moves beneath it.
2:30 Additional synthesizer layers — EMS Synthi AKS provides textural fills and atmospheric colours alongside the main sequence. The Synthi AKS (the briefcase version of the EMS Synthi A) is used for expressive ornamental elements throughout the album.
4:00 Melodic variation — subtle changes in the sequence and harmonic palette. The piece develops without abandoning its central pulse. Repetition-with-development: the minimalist strategy that would define electronic music for decades.
6:00 Peak complexity — maximum number of simultaneous elements. The apartment kitchen reaches its fullest imagined space. All tools of a one-man studio production: bouncing between tape machines, manual fader operation, no automation.
7:15 Fade toward Part III — the sequence releases. The melody prepared; Part III will reset the palette. Part II is complete as a formal statement. What follows constitutes the more experimental, abstract side of the album.

03 Oxygène Part III 3:16
Energy: Low Abstract Palette Cleanser EMS VCS 3
Album role: The album's briefest and most abstract section — an interlude between the two long melodic pieces either side of it. A palette-cleaning exploration using the EMS VCS 3's most unpredictable tonal qualities.
0:00 EMS VCS 3 experiment — unusual timbres, pitch-unstable tones. The most avant-garde moment on the album. The VCS 3 is a British analog synthesizer known for its unpredictable, wobbly oscillators — Jarre uses this instability as a feature.
1:30 Mid-section abstract textures — no melody, no conventional rhythm. Pure sound exploration. The shortest piece on the album is its most experimental. Its function is contrast. Side One ends here before the flip.
2:50 Transition to side boundary — the section dissolves. Original vinyl Side One ends after Part III. Side Two opens with Part IV — immediately establishing a new character. The palindromic structure of the album places its two most melodic pieces (II and IV) as the outer frame of the abstract centre.

04 Oxygène Part IV 4:14
Energy: Mid UK Hit Single Sequencer Pulse Most Accessible
Album role: The UK hit single and the album's most commercially accessible piece — the signature hi-hat pattern and driving sequencer pulse make this the most "pop" moment. Its relative brevity (only 4:14 among the longer album sections) gives it a focused, single-like economy of form.
0:00 Driving hi-hat pattern — Side Two opens with momentum — the Korg Mini-Pops 7 provides a continuous eighth-note hi-hat pulse. The album's most rhythmically propulsive opening. Compared to Part I's patient atmospheric build, Part IV arrives briskly and purposefully. Side Two makes different demands on the listener — more kinetic.
0:20 The Part IV melody enters — a different melodic vocabulary from Part II. More chromatic, slightly unsettling but still highly listenable. The melody that became the UK single in 1977. Its recognisability is distinct from Part II's sequence — more explicitly contoured as a tune.
1:30 Harmonic elaboration — the chord sequence under the melody adds depth. RMI Harmonic Synthesizer contributes to the harmonic texture. The RMI Harmonic Synthesizer is an electromechanical instrument — interesting timbral texture between organ and synthesizer.
2:30 Full arrangement, compositional peak — Part IV's complete production at its densest. The hit single earning its status. Chart position UK #65 on its original 1977 release — remarkable for an entirely instrumental electronic piece by a French composer.
3:45 Gradual reduction — the driving pulse eases. Part IV resolves cleanly before Part V's extended atmosphere. Part IV delivers the most compressed formal package on the album. Parts V and VI expand time again.

05 Oxygène Part V 10:23
Energy: Mid Longest Track Mellotron Evolving Panorama
Album role: The album's most expansive and diverse single section — over ten minutes traversing multiple textural territories. Part V contains the album's widest range of moods: meditative, urgent, ethereal, mechanical, and vast. The Mellotron appears here with its characteristic choral/orchestral illusion.
0:00 Expansive atmospheric opening — after Part IV's focused economy, Part V spreads outward. Wide synth pads, unhurried. The longest track earns its length by exploring what the shorter pieces could only suggest.
1:30 Mellotron enters — the strings/choir tape-replay instrument adds an orchestral warmth uniquely associated with 1970s progressive music. The Mellotron plays pre-recorded orchestral samples from tape — each key triggers a different tape strip. The result is recognisably artificial but hauntingly humanistic.
3:00 Rhythmic pulse develops — the Korg Mini-Pops reappears with a different pattern. The piece gains forward momentum. Multiple tempo zones within a single long section — the piece breathes differently in different moments.
5:00 Mid-section climax — the longest section reaches its highest intensity at midpoint. Multiple synthesizer voices active simultaneously. The cumulative density effect unique to Part V. No other section on the album sustains this much material for so long.
6:30 Deconstruction — elements remove themselves one by one. Long release into texture. The reverse of the opening build — a slow withdrawal. Jarre respects the listener's need for space.
8:00 Late-section re-emergence — a final melodic gesture, quieter, reflective. The piece finds a different character in its final two minutes — more inward, preparing for Part VI's elegant conclusion.
9:45 Transition to Part VI — the texture releases. The album's penultimate movement. Part V has covered the most ground. Part VI is the resolved, graceful landing.
BPM: Variable — Ambient to ~112 at peak
06 Oxygène Part VI 6:20
Energy: Low Closing Statement Graceful Resolution Harmonic Completion
Album role: The album's final statement — graceful, spacious, and harmonically resolving. Part VI returns to the atmospheric patience of Part I but now enriched by everything that has passed. The breath entering the body at the start now exhales fully.
0:00 Atmospheric resolution opens — unhurried, spacious. The album's accumulated tension releases into open sonic space. The final section mirrors the opening — both are atmospheric and melodically restrained. But Part VI carries all the preceding music's emotional weight.
1:20 Melodic farewell gesture — a long, slow melodic line on a synthesizer voice. Not the urgency of Part II or IV — contemplative departure. The final melody of the album is its most resigned and beautiful. There is no need to assert anything — the album has already said what it needed to say.
3:00 Part VI's single peak — modest by Part V's standards but emotionally fulfilling as a culminating gesture. The restraint of Part VI's peak is the point. This is the exhale after six parts of intake.
4:30 Final dissolution — the last notes extend into silence. The synthesizers thin one by one. The air of the album's title disperses back into the atmosphere from which it was summoned. Circular structure: begins in near-silence, ends in near-silence.
6:00 Album ends in silence — the last tone fades. 15–18 million copies worldwide. Grand Prix du Disque. Recorded alone in a Paris apartment. The silence at the end contains all of that.

Macro Arc — Album Structure

Side One: Preparation & Statement

Parts I–III form a progression from atmospheric patience (I) to iconic melodic statement (II) to brief abstract experiment (III). Side One's structure is: extended inhale → the main tune → abstract reset before the flip.

Parts I–III · ~18:44

Side Two: Pulse, Panorama & Resolution

Parts IV–VI move from the most accessible (IV, the single) through the most expansive (V, >10 minutes) to the graceful close (VI). Side Two earns its length by justifying every minute of Part V's exploration.

Parts IV–VI · ~20:57

The Apartment Kitchen Studio

Recorded without a budget, label, or studio, using a mixture of borrowed and affordable analog equipment. The recording environment's limitation — tape bouncing, manual fader automation — becomes central to the album's character: patient, precise, analogue-warm.

Rue de la Trémoille, Paris · 1976

Equipment Palette
